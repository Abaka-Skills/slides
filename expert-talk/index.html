Section 1 · The journey
Then the body takes over
  • The numbers are joint targets, not motion. Each joint closes its own gap.
  • An encoder measures the real angle; a PD controller compares it to the target, thousands of times per second.
  • The controller drives current into the motor; a gearbox turns it into torque.
  • Grippers are one motor each; the base turns velocity targets into wheel speeds.

Section 1 · How it learns · Imitation
Imitation — copy the demonstration
VID — step one, visible: a person drives the robot through the task, and every motion is recorded · Mobile ALOHA teleop, 10×
  • Step 1: a person demonstrates the task about 50 times, driving the robot by teleop.
  • Step 2: a network trains on the recordings — given what the cameras saw, output what the human did.
  • Step 3: the robot runs alone, reproducing the motions in slightly new conditions.
  • The limits: small errors drift into situations no demonstration covered, and the copy never beats the teacher.

Transfer — borrow what others learned
FIG — human hands teach a robot hand: transfer across bodies · EgoScale, Fig 1

Teleop — the robot records itself
VID — a person on the leader arms; the follower robot mirrors every move and records it · ALOHA, Zhao et al.
  • Leader-follower rigs: a person moves small leader arms, the robot mirrors, and every joint angle gets logged.
  • The result is exactly the data the policy needs: robot body, robot frame, real physics.
  • The cost side: one operator, one robot, real time. This is the pyramid's scarce top layer, hour by hour.

Section 1 · What it learns from · UMI
UMI — a gripper you carry around
VID — three collectors with UMI grippers, gathering demonstrations outdoors · Chi et al.
  • No robot on site. A person carries the gripper and just does the task, anywhere.
  • The camera's motion is tracked, and the gripper's path is reconstructed as if a robot had moved it.
  • Collection speed goes up an order of magnitude; kitchens, shops and homes all become collection sites.
  • The gap: no joint readings, and a hand-carried gripper moves differently than an arm.

Section 1 · What it learns from · Egocentric
Egocentric — people, just working
FIG — wearable capture: cameras and gloves record ordinary work · EgoScale, Fig 2
  • Head-mounted cameras, sometimes gloves for hand pose. People do their normal tasks; the footage is the data.
  • It scales like nothing else: hours accumulate as a side effect of daily life.
  • The hard part is the mapping. Human hands are not grippers, so every frame must be retargeted to robot joints. That step is where quality is won or lost.
  • This retargeting-and-QC work is our home ground. Section 4.
